SODA 308 Project Outline

1. Build the Singapore model

I want to study Singapore's development approach as a combination of choices. My theory is that a government can own or direct important industries and invest in the economy while staying open to global trade. How well the government can carry out those choices may affect how they work together.

I use four independent variable categories to represent this combination. I look at them together as a profile. For example, another economy might trade as much as Singapore does but invest less through its government. The profile lets me show which parts are similar and where the approaches differ.

What I mean by a similar model. Imagine two economies that are both open to trade. One also has substantial state ownership and government investment, while the other scores lower on both. I would call them similar on trade and different on the role of government. This gives me a way to compare the combination of choices without squeezing the whole approach into a single score.

What I expect. I want to see whether the relationship between policy choices and outcomes changes with government effectiveness. If I test this directly, I would compare the estimated relationship at different levels of effectiveness. The four measures give me a starting point for the comparison. Each represents a specific part of the approach.

2. Independent variables

  • State ownership. I picked this to capture how directly the government is involved in important industries. The main statistic is the V-Dem state ownership and control score from 0 to 4. Higher means more state control. State-owned enterprise employment or asset shares would provide another view if comparable data are available.
  • Government investment. I picked this to capture how much the government puts into fixed assets such as infrastructure. The main statistic is government fixed investment as a percentage of GDP. Public capital stock relative to GDP can show how much has accumulated over time.
  • Openness to trade. I picked this because Singapore's approach includes doing business with the rest of the world. The main statistic is exports plus imports as a percentage of GDP. Applied tariffs show one barrier to trade, and foreign direct investment inflows as a share of GDP show another form of international integration.
  • Government effectiveness. I picked this because having a policy and being able to carry it out are different things. The main statistic is the World Bank Government Effectiveness estimate. Regulatory quality and control of corruption are possible supporting measures.

How I read the four numbers together. State ownership tells me about control of important industries. Government investment tells me how much the government puts into fixed assets in a year. Trade tells me how connected the economy is to other markets. Government effectiveness tells me about its ability to carry out policy. I keep the units with each number because a point on an ownership scale means something different from a percentage point of GDP.

For the planned annual models, I would use the four predictors from the year before the outcome. For example, I would use 2018 predictors to look at 2019 growth. Each category has one main statistic. The supporting statistics give me another way to check a category, such as using tariffs to look at one trade policy alongside the amount of trade that actually takes place.

3. Dependent variables

These are the five areas I use to evaluate how the economies perform. I can show the individual statistics in each area.

  • Economic performance. Real GDP per person growth, GDP per person adjusted for purchasing power, unemployment, and output per worker.
  • Living standards. Life expectancy, infant mortality, secondary education attainment, and electricity access. The project also combines these into a living standards score when at least three are available.
  • Happiness. Average life evaluation on a 0–10 scale for a published reporting period. Positive and negative emotions are possible additions if comparable data are available.
  • Freedom. Civil liberties, freedom of expression and access to information, and freedom of association. The project also reports a score that combines these measures.
  • Social outcomes. Gender inequality, income inequality before and after taxes and transfers, poverty, and women's labor force participation relative to men's.

Why I keep the five areas separate. An economy can grow quickly while people rate their lives less positively than people elsewhere. It can also have high income per person and high income inequality. Looking at the outcomes separately lets me see where the same economy does well and where the picture is different.

When I show a combined score, I also show what goes into it. For living standards, for example, a reader should be able to see whether the comparison reflects life expectancy, education, electricity access, or several of those together. I compare life evaluation over the same published reporting period and keep the different social measures visible.

4. Build an average for each statistic

I start with one statistic and one year, or one shared reporting period for measures like happiness. I set aside the 11 focus economies and use the other economies that report that statistic for the same period. I add their values and divide by the number reporting. Each reporting economy counts once.

I use that same average for all 11 focus economies. I show how far each economy is above or below it in the statistic's own units. I rebuild the average for each statistic because the group that reports GDP per person may differ from the group that reports poverty. I show how many economies went into each average and flag comparisons with fewer than 100 outside economies.

For example, in 2019, GDP per person adjusted for purchasing power averaged about 25,991 international dollars across 188 outside economies. Singapore was about 94,687 international dollars above that average. For infant mortality, a value below the average would indicate a better outcome.

How I would show the comparison. For each statistic, I would put the focus economy's value, the outside mean, the gap, and the number of outside economies in the same table. Each of the 11 gets its own row. The mean and reporting count stay the same across those 11 rows for that statistic and year.

How I interpret the gap. A positive gap means the value is above the mean. Whether that is a good outcome depends on the statistic. Higher life expectancy usually points to better health, while higher infant mortality points to worse health. For a predictor such as state ownership, the gap tells me how the policy profile differs. If an economy has no value for a statistic that year, I show it as missing.

The GDP example shows the outside benchmark calculation with actual data. I would repeat that calculation separately for the other statistics and show which comparisons have enough outside data.

5. How the repository works

I use the repository to show where my numbers come from and how I turn them into results. A reader can start with a graph, find the table behind it, and then check how the R scripts built that table.

  1. Get the data. The R scripts download or reuse the source files. I keep the original downloads in my local folder and use the source records to track where the data came from. Source notes
  2. Clean and match. I match records by economy and year, or by reporting period when the source covers several years. I keep the data used by R and make copies with headings that are easier to read. Data guide
  3. Make the comparisons. The scripts calculate averages, build scores, and estimate the existing models. The outside average in this outline is illustrated with the GDP example. Extending that comparison to the other statistics and running the revised four-predictor models are separate steps. R workflow
  4. Open and check the results. I use the graphs and labeled tables to explain what I found. The checks help catch problems such as missing files, mismatched years, and broken links. Open the results catalog

To rebuild the saved results, I open the SODA 308 project in RStudio and run source("run_all.R"). To share an update, I review the files and use the manual GitHub sync. Manual sync instructions
